Chapters and tasks

    Find the angle where the shadow is longest.

    Principal component analysis (PCA) describes data with fewer numbers while losing as little as possible. Before any math, try it by hand. Each dot is one day: the high temperature in two neighboring towns. Turn the line and every dot casts a shadow onto it.

    How to use this guide: every figure has a short Try this list. Do the tasks in order. The note under each list reacts to what you do, and the chapter list on the left fills in as you finish tasks.

    1

    Why describe data with fewer numbers?

    A dimension is one column in your table: one feature. The temperature data has two. Real ML datasets often have hundreds: pixels in an image, words in a document, sensor readings, survey answers.

    Many of those columns repeat each other. If Town A is hot, Town B is almost always hot too, so the second column tells you little that the first did not. Dimensionality reduction finds a smaller set of new columns that keeps most of the information. That helps you:

    • See high-dimensional data as a 2D or 3D plot.
    • Train faster, because models have fewer inputs to process.
    • Fight overfitting, since fewer inputs means fewer ways to memorize noise.
    • Remove redundancy between features that move together.

    Whether one number can stand in for two depends on how much the features repeat each other. Use the slider to control that.

    That is the whole idea of PCA: find the directions where the data spreads out most, keep the strong ones, and drop the weak ones. The rest of this guide shows the five steps that find those directions with arithmetic instead of a slider.

    2

    Step 1. Center the data

    PCA looks for directions of spread around the middle of the data. So first, move the middle to zero. For each feature, compute its mean and subtract it from every value:

    x̄ = 1n ( x1 + x2 + … + xn )    then    xi ← xi − x̄ Here n is the number of rows (40 days) and x̄ is read “x-bar”, the mean.

    Centering slides the cloud without stretching or turning it, so the shape (the thing PCA cares about) stays the same. From here on, every plot shows centered data.

    3

    Step 2. Measure how the features move together

    Variance measures how spread out one feature is: the average squared distance from the mean.

    Var(x) = 1n − 1 Σ xi2 The data is already centered, so each xi is a distance from the mean. Dividing by n − 1 instead of n is a small correction for estimating from a sample.

    Covariance asks a two-feature question: when x is above its mean, is y above its mean too? Multiply the two deviations for each point and average:

    Cov(x, y) = 1n − 1 Σ xi · yi

    Here is a way to see that product. For each point, draw a rectangle from the origin to the point. Its area is xi · yi. Points in the top-right or bottom-left give positive areas (blue). Points in the other two corners give negative areas (orange). Covariance is the net area, averaged.

    Put every variance and covariance into a grid and you get the covariance matrix, usually written C or Σ. With d features it is a d × d table:

    • The diagonal holds each feature's variance (its covariance with itself).
    • The off-diagonal cells hold covariances between pairs of features.
    • It is symmetric: Cov(x, y) = Cov(y, x), so the top-right mirrors the bottom-left.

    With the data stacked as a matrix X (one row per day, one column per feature), the whole grid comes from one line: C = XTX / (n − 1).

    Did you notice what happened while you tilted? The two variances traded places, but their sum stayed fixed. Turning the cloud moves spread between axes; it never creates or destroys it. PCA uses that fact: it turns the axes so as much spread as possible lands on the first one.

    4

    Covariance or correlation?

    Covariance has a catch: its size depends on your units. Measure a length in millimeters instead of centimeters and every covariance involving it grows tenfold, and its variance grows a hundredfold. Nothing about the data changed.

    Correlation fixes this by dividing out each feature's own spread (its standard deviation, σ, the square root of its variance):

    r(x, y) = Cov(x, y)σx · σy The result has no units and always lies between −1 and 1. It is the same as the covariance you get after standardizing each feature: subtracting the mean and dividing by σ, which gives z-scores.

    To see why this matters for PCA, switch to a dataset with mismatched units: 60 penguins, with flipper length and body mass. They are strongly related (bigger penguins have longer flippers), so the honest answer is that PC1 should be a blend of both.

    5

    Step 3. Find the directions the matrix does not turn

    Back to the temperatures. You found PC1 in Figure 1 by turning a line. The math finds it in one shot, using eigenvectors.

    Think of the covariance matrix C as a machine: feed it an arrow v, and it hands back a new arrow Cv. Most arrows come out turned. A few special directions come out pointing the same way, only longer or shorter:

    C v = λ v The arrow v is an eigenvector. The stretch factor λ (lambda) is its eigenvalue.

    For a covariance matrix, these special directions are exactly the principal components, and each eigenvalue is the variance of the data along its direction. Find them below.

    For a 2 × 2 matrix you can solve this by hand. These numbers come from the temperature data:

      Why does the eigenvector give the longest shadow? The variance of the shadows along a unit direction u works out to uTCu. Making that as big as possible, while keeping u at length 1, leads straight to Cu = λu. The biggest λ wins. Real libraries use faster methods (often the singular value decomposition), but they compute the same directions.

      6

      Step 4. Rotate onto the new axes, then drop the weak ones

      Sort the eigenvectors by eigenvalue, largest first. Each one becomes a new axis. A point's coordinate on a new axis, called its score, is its shadow length: the dot product with that eigenvector.

      score1 = xi · v1 = xi,A v1,A + xi,B v1,B

      The new axes also mean something. PC1 weights both towns about equally, so a high PC1 score means a warm day in both towns. PC2 weights them with opposite signs, so it measures how much warmer Town A was than Town B. Two correlated columns became one strong column (overall warmth) and one weak one (the difference). Keeping only PC1 halves the data and keeps of the variance.

      In matrix form, with the chosen eigenvectors as the columns of W, the whole projection is Z = XW. Each row of Z is one day, described with fewer numbers.

      7

      Step 5. Decide how many components to keep

      With two features the choice is easy. With many, you need a rule. Each eigenvalue divided by the sum of all eigenvalues is the share of variance that component explains. Plot those shares in order and you get a scree plot.

      This dataset has 80 cars and six features in different units, so PCA runs on the correlation matrix. Below the chart is that matrix: a 6 × 6 grid, the bigger version of the 2 × 2 grids you used earlier.

      Common rules for choosing k:

      • Cumulative threshold: keep enough components to explain 90 to 95% of the variance.
      • Elbow: keep the components before the bars flatten out.
      • Downstream score: try several values of k and keep the one that makes your model perform best on validation data.
      8

      The whole recipe, and a quick check

      1. Center each feature by subtracting its mean. If features use different units, also divide by the standard deviation (standardize).
      2. Build the matrix: C = XTX / (n − 1). That is the covariance matrix of centered data, or the correlation matrix of standardized data.
      3. Find eigenvectors and eigenvalues of C. Eigenvectors are the principal directions; eigenvalues are the variance along each.
      4. Sort and choose k using explained variance: each λ divided by the sum of all λ.
      5. Project: Z = XW, where W holds the top k eigenvectors as columns.

      Things that trip people up

      • Scale: unscaled features with big numbers dominate. You saw PC1 swing between features just by changing grams to kilograms.
      • Outliers: covariance squares and multiplies distances, so a single extreme point can tilt the components. You dragged one dot and watched it happen.
      • Straight lines only: PCA finds flat directions. Data on a curve (a spiral, a ring) needs nonlinear methods such as kernel PCA, t-SNE, or UMAP.
      • Signs are arbitrary: if v is an eigenvector, so is −v. Two libraries can return flipped components that mean the same thing.
      • Fit on training data only: compute the means, scales, and components from the training set, then apply them to the test set. Fitting on everything leaks test information.
      • Meaning gets blurry: each component is a blend of features. Read the loadings, as you did for the cars, before naming one.
